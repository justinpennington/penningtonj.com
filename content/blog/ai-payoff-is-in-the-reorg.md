title: "The AI Payoff Is in the Reorg, Not the Rollout"
date: "2026-11-24"
excerpt: "Anthropic interviewed 58 of its enterprise customers. The companies getting the most from AI changed how the work is organized, not just which tools people have."
---

Anthropic published a report on November 23 called *Working with AI*. Its economics team ran 66 interviews with people at 58 of its US enterprise customers: law firms, dentists, accountants, hedge funds, manufacturers, a property manager, a trucking company. They looked closely at nearly 300 specific AI use cases and asked two questions. How much value did this create? And how much did the organization have to change to get it?

The answer lines up with what I see every week. The light-touch uses, like ad hoc data analysis or building a slide deck, fit into the existing org chart, and many of the simplest ones were rated relatively low value by the people using them. The highest-value uses involved real reorganization: broader roles, people owning whole workflows, and staff moved onto the tasks AI still can't do.

## What changed

Most of the conversation about AI at work is still about tools. Which model, which seat license, which copilot. This report is a useful correction.

Interviewees said most AI uses added value by increasing output, not by cutting costs. Very few companies had laid anyone off because of AI; only two described automation fully eliminating a position. But people at two-thirds of the companies said they were hiring less, and many had cut back on contractors, especially offshore ones.

The report is upfront about its limits. The companies were picked for range, not at random, and the value ratings are mostly what interviewees described, not measured results. Read it as field notes, not a benchmark. The field notes are still specific enough to learn from.

## Why it matters for operators

Two stories in the report stuck with me.

One company built an agent that reads submissions against its approval policies and hands a structured analysis to human reviewers. It planned to cut the number of reviewers it expected to hire by more than 90%. The reviewers balked. As one executive put it, if a reviewer can now handle double the cases, how do you get them to actually do it? The tool worked. The job design didn't.

The other is a trucking company that built an agent-accessible data warehouse. Managers across the company started running their own analyses instead of waiting on a report. Someone there said they're "absolutely addicted to it."

That second story is the pattern. The win wasn't a smarter model. It was putting the business's data where both people and agents could reach it, then letting the people closest to the work ask their own questions.

The report flags one more thing worth planning for: as coding speeds up, tasks like code review, sales and strategic decisions increasingly have to keep pace. Making one step faster just moves the wait to the next one.

## My take

If you run a small or mid-size business and your AI plan is "give everyone a subscription," expect the low-value corner of that chart. Some faster emails. Some nicer decks.

The work that pays starts with a different question: now that the busywork inside a workflow is cheap, who should own that workflow end to end? Then fix whatever blocks that person. Usually it's data spread across five systems, approvals that live in someone's inbox, or a handoff nobody owns.

That's most of what we build at Infraxio. IFX Hub puts operations, customer records and documents in one place so a person, or an agent, can see the whole job instead of one step of it. It's less exciting than a new model release. It's also where this report says the value is.

Before you buy another tool, pick one workflow, name the owner, and redraw it around what AI can now do. That's the reorg. The rollout is the easy part.
