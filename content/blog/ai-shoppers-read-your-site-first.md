Today is Black Friday, and the number I keep coming back to isn't the sales total. It's where the shoppers are coming from.

Adobe forecasts that traffic to US retail sites from AI chat and browser tools will be up 95% year over year today, after a forecast 159% jump on Thanksgiving, with Cyber Monday expected up 88%. For the full season (Nov. 1 to Dec. 31), Adobe expects AI traffic to rise 130%. As of this week, Adobe had already seen AI-referred visits up 127% so far this season, according to Digital Commerce 360. Adobe measures this as shoppers clicking a link from an AI service through to a retailer.

## What changed

A year ago this traffic was a curiosity. In August 2025, Adobe says visitors arriving from AI sources converted 9% worse than everyone else. By September 2026, they converted 90% better than non-AI traffic, spent 73% more time on site, viewed 25% more pages and added items to carts at a 53% higher rate. Revenue per visit was 83% higher.

That makes sense when you think about how people use these tools. The research happens in the chat. By the time someone clicks through, the comparing is mostly done. They're showing up to buy.

## The part most businesses miss

The visitor arrives already holding an answer about you: your price, your return policy, whether you ship in time, whether you're open Saturday. The AI gave them that answer before they ever reached your site.

So the real question is what the AI was able to read.

Adobe has a measure for this it calls a Citation Readability Score: how much of a page's content AI systems can actually understand and cite. In September, the average US retail homepage scored 63%, which means more than a third of homepage content wasn't visible to those systems. Electronics led at 79%, carried by category and customer service pages full of setup guides, specs and troubleshooting. General merchandise homepages scored 40%. Those are the promotional, navigation-heavy layouts.

Adobe's conclusion is plain: the content AI reads best is content that explains.

## My take for operators

Adobe's numbers are about retail, but I don't think the lesson stops there. If you run a service business, a distribution company or a contractor shop, somebody is already asking an AI tool about your pricing, your service area and your turnaround time. Whatever it says is the first impression.

Here's what I'd do this weekend, before Cyber Monday:

1. **Ask the tools about yourself.** Open two or three AI assistants and ask what you charge, what your hours are and what your return or cancellation policy is. Write down what's wrong.
2. **Put the answers in text.** Prices, policies, hours, lead times and service areas belong in plain text on a real page, not in a banner image or a PDF.
3. **Have one source of truth.** If your website says one thing, your listings say another and your staff quote a third, the AI will pick one. You won't get a say in which.
4. **Track AI referrals as their own channel.** If you can't see that traffic separately in your analytics, you can't tell whether it's worth more than your paid search.

None of this is new SEO advice dressed up. It's the same discipline we use at Infraxio when we build systems for clients: the data has to live in one place, be current and be readable by whoever needs it. The difference now is that "whoever" includes software that's talking to your customer before you are.

There's one more thing worth keeping in perspective. In a YouGov survey of British adults cited by CX Network, 40% of planned Black Friday shoppers said they'd be comfortable using AI to find the best price, but only 7% would let it add items to a basket and 5% would let it complete a purchase. People are handing AI the research, not the decision. That's good news, because it means the click still comes to you. Just make sure what they were told before the click is true.
