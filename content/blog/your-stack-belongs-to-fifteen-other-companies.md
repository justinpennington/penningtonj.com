Every operator I talk to has a version of the same story. Fifteen SaaS subscriptions. Fifteen logins. Fifteen vendor relationships. Fifteen sets of terms, pricing tiers, and roadmaps that were never designed to fit each other. The collection gets called "our stack," as though choosing fifteen tools were the same as owning a system.

It is not. Every tool in that stack belongs to the company that built it. They set the price. They choose what ships next. They decide when to sunset the feature you depend on. You are renting pieces of your operation from fifteen landlords who do not talk to each other.

I run [Infraxio](https://www.infraxio.com) from Ponte Vedra, and the products we have built — [IFX Hub](https://ifxhub.io), [Growth7](https://www.growth7.io), [IFX Bid](https://www.ifxbid.com), [DockOps](https://www.dockops.app) — exist because we got tired of being tenants in our own operation.

## A collection is not a system

Fifteen SaaS products in a row is not a system. It is a collection. A system has shared context, connected workflows, and a single source of truth about what happened and what comes next. A collection has exports, imports, and the person on your team who knows which CSV goes where.

The distinction matters the moment something breaks. In a system, a problem shows up where the problem lives. In a collection, a problem surfaces three tools downstream, and the person who finds it has to walk the chain backward through platforms they may not even have access to.

I wrote before about [the integration layer nobody plans for](/blog/the-integration-layer-nobody-plans-for). The SaaS stack is the other side of that problem. The integration layer is hard not because APIs are complicated. It is hard because you are trying to make fifteen independent products behave like one system, and none of them were designed to cooperate.

## The invisible cost of renting everything

The visible cost of a SaaS stack is the sum of the subscription lines. The invisible cost is everything the subscriptions create.

Someone on the team manages vendor relationships. Someone tracks renewal dates and watches for pricing changes. Someone handles credential rotation when a team member leaves. Someone maintains the Zapier chains or custom integrations that connect tools. Someone reconciles the data when two products disagree about the same customer, the same order, or the same dollar amount.

That "someone" is often the same person, and that person's actual job is supposed to be running the operation — not babysitting software.

Then there is the strategic cost. Every vendor makes roadmap decisions based on their largest customer segment. If your workflow depends on a feature that serves a niche corner of their market, that feature will eventually be deprioritized, redesigned, or removed. You find out in a changelog you did not read. [The handoff is where revenue leaks](/blog/the-handoff-is-where-revenue-leaks), and every vendor boundary in your stack is a handoff you do not control.

## The pricing move you cannot plan for

SaaS pricing is designed to grow with you — which sounds fair until you realize that "grow with you" means "charge you more precisely when the tool is most embedded in your operation." By the time a tool is genuinely critical, you have years of data in it, workflows built around it, and a team trained on its interface. That is the moment the per-seat price goes up, the free tier disappears, or the plan you rely on gets rolled into a more expensive one.

This is not a conspiracy. It is how the model works. The product gets stickier over time, and the price adjusts to match. The company that holds your data and your integrations holds the leverage. Your switching cost is their pricing power.

## What changes when you own the core

When we built Hub, we were not trying to replace every tool in the market. We were solving a specific problem: the post-sale workflow — from closed deal through delivery to invoice — was scattered across products that did not share context. The person who held the thread was an operations lead with a spreadsheet and a memory that the business could not afford to lose.

Hub is the core: deals, delivery, tasks, invoicing, and client records, in one place. Not because consolidation is a philosophy, but because the handoffs between separate tools were where work fell apart and context disappeared. [Nobody lives in the CRM](/blog/nobody-lives-in-the-crm) when the CRM is a side trip from where the real work happens. People live in whatever system is closest to their actual job. If that system belongs to a vendor whose priorities do not match your operation, your team works around it — and enough workarounds turn the system into decoration.

Growth7 connects growth work forward into operations so leads do not stall in the gap between marketing and delivery. IFX Bid treats procurement response as a coordinated workflow instead of a shared drive of drafts. DockOps puts dock scheduling, carrier check-in, and yard tracking where clipboard-and-radio used to live. Each exists because a rented tool could not change when the operation needed it to.

[Buying or building misses the real question](/blog/buying-or-building-misses-the-real-question). The real question is who gets to change the system next month when the business changes. If the answer is a vendor's product team in another city working from a backlog you will never see, you are on their timeline.

## Not everything needs to be owned

I am not arguing that every business should build everything from scratch. That would be absurd. Email hosting, file storage, basic accounting — those are mature commodity services where renting is the obvious move. The switching cost is manageable, the workflows are standard, and no competitive advantage lives in how you send email.

The argument is about the core. Whatever sits at the center of how your team does its actual work — the system that holds client context, manages delivery, tracks revenue, and connects the people who depend on each other — that is the piece where ownership matters. That is where the next change will come from, and where a vendor's roadmap will not be waiting for you.

## The shape of a stack you can actually run

A workable stack has a small number of owned or deeply controlled systems at the center, surrounded by commodity services at the edges. The core holds the workflows that make your business yours. The edges handle the utilities.

We run [Infraxio](https://www.infraxio.com) on that shape. Seven people, products funded from revenue and partnerships, never venture capital. The products were built because we needed them first and because we were unwilling to wait for fifteen vendors to independently decide to solve the same workflow we were already living in.

From Ponte Vedra, the companies I work with across St. Johns County and Jacksonville show the same pattern. The stack grew tool by tool, each solving a problem the last one did not quite cover. Nobody planned the collection. Nobody owns it now. The operator who holds it together is the most expensive integration layer in the building — and they know it, even if nobody else does.

Start with the core. Own or control the system your team actually works in every day. Let the edges stay rented. And stop calling fifteen logins a strategy.
