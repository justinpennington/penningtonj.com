I have walked into dozens of businesses that proudly tell me they have a CRM, and then I open it and find a spreadsheet with a login screen. Contacts with no activity history. Deals with no stages. Pipelines that exist in name only because nobody moves cards.

## What a CRM Should Actually Do

A CRM is a system of record for every interaction your business has with a prospect or customer. Every call, every email, every meeting, every proposal, every invoice. When a salesperson opens a contact, they should see the full story: when the last conversation happened, what was discussed, what was promised, and what the next step is. When a manager looks at the pipeline, they should see real probabilities based on activity, not gut feelings.

## The Data Hygiene Problem

Most CRM problems are data problems. Duplicate contacts, missing fields, stale opportunities that should have been closed-lost six months ago. The fix is not a cleanup project; it is a process change. Every CRM needs rules: what fields are required before a record can be saved, what triggers move a deal from one stage to the next, and who is responsible for data quality in each segment.

## Connecting CRM to Operations

The real power of a CRM shows up when it connects to the rest of your operations. When a deal closes, does the system automatically create a project, generate an invoice, and notify the delivery team? If the answer is no, you have a gap that costs you time and creates errors every single day.

## Start With Outcomes

If your CRM feels like a chore, ask yourself what outcome you want from it. If the answer is "I want to know exactly where every deal stands and what my team needs to do today," then work backwards from that outcome and strip away everything that does not serve it.
