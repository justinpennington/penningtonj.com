A customer asks what the work will cost. Nothing has been done yet. For a contractor, a marine or yacht service shop, a field crew, a distributor, or a logistics operator, the quote is the first thing they actually receive.

They read the turnaround, the scope, the exclusions, and whether the next person describes the same job. A clear quote, sent while the request is still warm, says the operation can hold a commitment. A late or shifting one says it cannot.

I run [Infraxio](https://www.infraxio.com) from Ponte Vedra. Around St. Johns County, Jacksonville, and the rest of Northeast Florida, quoting is usually the least designed step: one person's head, a spreadsheet template, and an email. Nobody owns the turnaround. Open quotes wait on memory. A yes gets retyped into a work order.

## The Buyer Is Judging the Operation

A slow quote sat on one person. A quote that changes under a second set of eyes has no shared rules. A quote that skips travel, materials, access, or what you will not do hands the customer a surprise later, in a change order or an argument. They read the estimate as a preview of the job.

They are deciding whether the job will be scoped, scheduled, and billed the way it was described. If this quote is late, vague, or different from the last one for the same kind of work, they assume the rest of the operation matches.

## Write the Rules Where the Quote Is Built

Most quoting systems are a person. They know which work you take, the labor assumption, the material markup, the exclusions that belong on the page, and which customers get a different posture. The spreadsheet is last month's file. The logic is a conversation. It holds until that person is on a job, out, or gone.

This is [a business that runs on what one person remembers](/blog/your-business-runs-on-what-one-person-remembers), pointed at the price. Similar jobs come back at different numbers, and nobody can explain the gap.

Write the rules in the place the quote is built.

**Included by default.** Labor, standard materials, travel inside a defined area, the ordinary scope for that kind of job.

**Always called out.** Permits, after-hours work, access, customer-supplied parts, anything that moves the price if it shows up on site.

**What you will not do.** Exclusions, work that belongs to someone else, conditions that void the number.

**What the number assumes.** Site conditions, quantities, lead times, and how long the price holds.

A second person can then send a quote the first person would recognize. The person who knows the work still sets the posture.

## A Won Quote Should Become the Job

The leak opens when the customer says yes.

Scope is retyped into a work order. Materials land on another list. The terms never reach the job. The crew gets a title and an address, and the invoice comes from a different sheet or from memory.

That is the handoff in [where revenue leaks](/blog/the-handoff-is-where-revenue-leaks). Estimating looks finished. Delivery and billing each start from a thinner copy. A retype can drop a line, change a quantity, or lose an exclusion the customer already accepted.

A won quote should become the job with the scope, the materials, and the terms intact. No second entry.

The invoice is that same record, moved forward.

## Follow-Up Is a System

Open quotes die quietly. The customer got a number and then nothing, or a question sat in one inbox. The estimator meant to circle back. The week filled up.

The advice to call harder lands on the person who is also pricing the next job and answering the phone. Wanting it more does not outlast a full board.

Follow-up on open quotes is a growth system problem. Someone has to see which quotes are still open, how long they have sat, and what the next touch is, including on the days the estimator is on a site.

[Growth7](https://www.growth7.io) is where we put that work. A quote that has not moved gets a follow-up sequence and shows up as stalled, the same way an unanswered lead does. The estimator still owns the relationship and the number. The system owns the reminder and the list of what is sitting.

## A Formal Bid Is the Same Job, Louder

A public-sector solicitation or a formal package is the same job with the consequences turned up: a longer scope, a public deadline, and a price you will be held to. A missing exclusion is now a compliance problem.

Pursue or pass happens in a hallway. Language sits in old folders. Price sits with one person. The package is assembled at the end, and a win still hands delivery a PDF.

## Use AI on the Draft, Not on the Promise

AI earns a place on a quote after the rules and the past jobs are in the system. A chat window that invents a price does not.

With that history, a model can draft scope language from jobs you have already done, so the estimator edits instead of starting from a blank template. It can check the draft against the pricing rules and flag a missing exclusion, a material line that breaks an assumption, or a term this kind of job usually carries. A missing exclusion is how a fair price becomes a dispute.

A person still owns the price and the commitments. The model does not know the site, the customer, or what you are willing to promise this week. Draft, check, and flag. The estimator sends it.

## Questions for This Week

Take this week's quotes and ask:

**Who else can price a job when the usual person is out?** If the answer is nobody, the price list is a memory.

**Where are the rules, the assumptions, and the exclusions written?** If they live in a sent folder, they are examples, not rules.

**What gets retyped when a quote is accepted?** Scope, materials, terms. Each retype is a place the job can drift from what the customer bought.

**Which open quotes have no next touch, and who can see that list without asking?** If it lives in one inbox, follow-up is a mood.

**Would you hand the customer this quote as a description of how the job will be run?** They are already reading it that way.

The number is what they compare. The quote is the first thing they buy. Make it the same record the rest of the company will work from, with the rules written down and a person still responsible for the promise.
