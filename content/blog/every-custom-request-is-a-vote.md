The request shows up as a small thing. One field on the quote. One extra stage. A report that matches the spreadsheet they used last year. The person asking is a customer you want to keep, and someone on the team says we can do it this once.

That sentence is how a product becomes a stack of private versions.

I run [Infraxio](https://www.infraxio.com) from Ponte Vedra. An operator in St. Johns County or Jacksonville has outgrown the tools, and we either put the work on a product or build the system that work actually needs. The hard part is the next request, once the same product is running in more than one company.

## A Request Is a Vote

In a services engagement, the client is paying for the thing they described. When that work turns into a product, the request changes jobs. It is evidence, not an order.

One customer wants a button. That tells you a job is unfinished. It does not tell you the button belongs in the product for everyone else. CRM and ERP projects have been teaching this for a long time. The customization that closed the deal becomes the reason the company cannot take the next release. Agencies that grow software out of client work hit the same wall with their own code. I wrote about how our products started as [patterns we kept rebuilding](/blog/from-client-work-to-products). After that, the work is telling a pattern from one company's habit.

Write the job down before anyone opens a branch. Who is stuck, what they are trying to finish, and what they do today when the product will not do it. A field, a template, a stage, or a permission may already cover it. Sometimes the job belongs to them alone. Skip that line and the next customer with the same job asks for a different button, which you will build too.

## Three Piles

Every request goes in one of three piles. Mixing them is how you get a fork.

**Configuration.** The product should already allow this. A setting, a field, a template, a stage, a permission. The product was supposed to bend for this without a code change. Fix the setting once, so any customer can turn it on or leave it off. The next ask is a checkbox, not a project.

**A gap with more than one vote.** The job is real, the product cannot express it, and the same job has shown up from more than one customer. That earns a place on the roadmap. One request is a story. A second, from a different company, is a pattern. The second customer rarely wants the same button. They want the same outcome, so write the job before you draw the screen.

**A one-off.** One company, one process, no sign anyone else works this way. That belongs in a separate custom build, scoped and paid as custom work, or in a polite no. It does not belong on the product branch. They can still be a good customer for a custom system. They are a bad reason to fork what everyone else runs.

If you cannot name the pile, you do not know enough to write the code.

## What Just This Once Costs

The build is the part people remember. Someone adds the field, the customer is happy, and the conversation moves on. The cost shows up when the main product moves and the exception has to come with it.

A one-off branch has to be merged, or it drifts. Every fix on the shared product has to be checked against the private screen. The person who knew why it existed moves on, and the branch stays, because taking it out might surprise a customer. Just this once is how a second codebase shows up without anyone deciding to own one.

The next exception is easier, because the first one proved you will say yes. Each branch is small. Together they are a release: test the shared product, test the exceptions, remember which customer is on which version. That maintenance does not shrink because the original change was small.

The same shape shows up in CRM and ERP projects. The vendor's product was fine. The implementation added objects, scripts, and screens until a release required a project of its own. The login still carried the vendor's name, but it was no longer the product they bought. Configuration over customization is the rule: if the business can live in settings, put it in settings. Spend code on what the product cannot be.

That is also why [one platform](/blog/the-case-for-one-platform) only holds if it stays one. A pile of tools and a spreadsheet is one split. A private branch per customer is another. Both leave a person maintaining the seams.

## Where It Shows Up in the Work

[IFX Hub](https://ifxhub.io) is deployed per client, which is where the temptation lives. Stages, fields, and permissions should be configuration. A code path only that deployment runs is a private copy. A workflow no other company will run is a custom system, and it should be quoted that way.

[Growth7](https://www.growth7.io) gets the same pressure on the marketing side. A sequence step, a field on the lead, an approval that matches how a team already decides. A template or a stage belongs in the product for the next account. A mechanic for a single account is a service, not a feature the product carries after they move on.

[IFX Bid](https://www.ifxbid.com) collects requests that sound unique and often are not. A cover sheet, a compliance section, a stage before the package goes out. Most of that is templates and stages. A scoring habit no other bidder uses can be configuration if the knobs exist. It should not become a fork so one team can keep a column from an old sheet.

[DockOps](https://www.dockops.app) gets the yard-specific ask. One more status, one door rule, a report a scheduler kept on paper. Every yard has exceptions, so a yard has to describe its own. One site's habit is different. Writing the job down separates a need to hold a door from a request to recreate a column off a clipboard.

Custom systems sit beside the products on purpose. When the work is genuinely theirs, we build it as theirs. It does not ride the product's release, and the product does not inherit its exceptions.

## Faster to Build Is Not Cheaper to Keep

AI-assisted development changed the afternoon. A field or a small branch that used to be scheduled work can show up the same morning. The diff is short, the customer is waiting, and it feels like a reason to say yes.

The model writes the change faster. It does not sit in the maintenance, and it does not remember which customer needed the private stage or what breaks when the shared workflow moves. Someone still has to carry the one-off through the next release. None of that got cheaper because a model typed the first version.

The speed makes the bad yes easier. The cost shows up when two branches disagree and someone has to decide which customer's version is the product. By then the ask is a memory, and the branch is a fact.

Count the votes before you build. If the job is configuration, build the setting and leave each customer's version as a choice. If more than one customer is describing the same job, put it on the roadmap and design it once. If it is one company, put it in a custom build with its own scope, or say no and keep the product shared.

The next request will still arrive as a small favor. Write the job on one line before you open the branch. Then put it in a pile. The pile is the decision.
