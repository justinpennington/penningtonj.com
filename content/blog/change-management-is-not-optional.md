I have seen beautifully implemented systems sit unused because no one invested in helping the people who were supposed to use them through the transition. The technology was right. The change management was absent.

## What Change Management Actually Means

Change management is not a motivational speech at the kickoff meeting. It is a structured process for helping people move from their current way of working to a new way of working. It includes communication (what is changing and why), involvement (giving users a voice in the process), training (teaching the new workflows), and support (being available when things go wrong).

## Resistance Is Information

When users resist a new system, it is tempting to label them as difficult or resistant to change. But resistance is usually information. It means the system does not fit their workflow, the training was insufficient, or they were not involved in the decisions that shaped the tool they now have to use. Listening to resistance is more productive than overriding it.

## The Champion Network

The most effective change management approach I have seen is a champion network: identify two or three people in each department who are early adopters, train them first, and let them become the go-to resource for their peers. People trust their colleagues more than they trust a consultant or a training video.

## Measuring Change

Change management success is measurable. Track login frequency, workflow completion rates, support ticket volume, and workaround usage. If logins are low, people are not using the system. If support tickets spike on a specific feature, the training for that feature was inadequate. If workarounds persist, the system is not meeting a need that should be addressed.
